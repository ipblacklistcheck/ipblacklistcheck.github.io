Writing a Blacklist Removal Request That Gets Approved

A good delisting request is short, honest, and specific. Learn what operators want to see, what to include, and the mistakes that get requests refused.

What operators actually want

List operators receive thousands of removal requests, and they approve the ones that show the problem was understood and fixed. They are not looking for a long apology or a claim of innocence. They want to know what happened, what changed, and why it will not happen again. A request that names the cause, gives dates, and lists concrete fixes is easy to approve. A request that says nothing went wrong, or that blames the operator, is easy to refuse. Remember that the operator's job is to protect mail recipients, not to do you a favour, so write from that perspective and show that recipients are now safe.

Structure your request clearly

Start with the IP address, the list name, and the date you found the listing. Then describe the cause in one or two sentences, for example a compromised mailbox that was sending spam between two dates. Then list the fixes as bullet points: password changed, two-factor authentication enabled, malicious script removed, software updated, open relay closed. Then state when the unwanted traffic stopped and what monitoring you have added to catch a repeat. End with a polite request for removal and a contact address you monitor. Keep the whole request under a few hundred words. Operators appreciate brevity, and a short, factual request is more credible than a long, emotional one.

Evidence beats promises

Where the operator allows attachments or log excerpts, include brief evidence that supports your story: a log line showing the spam stopping, a screenshot of the patched setting, or a scan result showing the site is clean. Do not send passwords, full log files, or unrelated personal data. If the cause was a compromised account, say which account and when it was secured. If the cause was a misconfiguration, name the setting that was wrong and its correct value now. Evidence turns a promise into a verifiable fact, and verifiable facts are what get requests approved.

Mistakes that get requests refused

The commonest mistake is requesting removal before fixing the cause. If spam is still flowing, the operator will refuse and may note the attempt. Another mistake is submitting the same generic request to every operator without reading each one's instructions, or using a removal service that sends a templated message. Threatening legal action, claiming the listing is defamation, or arguing that the operator has no right to list you almost always backfires. Operators are private services with the right to publish their data. A calm, factual request that respects their process is far more effective than any confrontation.

If your request is refused

Read the refusal carefully, because it usually names the remaining problem. It may say that spam is still being seen, that reverse DNS is missing, or that a policy issue remains. Fix that specific problem, wait for the period the operator states, and submit a fresh request that addresses the refusal directly. Do not resubmit the same request immediately, and do not submit multiple requests in the hope that one will slip through. If you cannot identify the problem, ask your hosting provider or a mail administrator for help, and consider a reputable deliverability consultant for a difficult case. Persistence with fixes works; persistence without fixes does not.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a removal request be?

A few hundred words at most: the IP, the cause, the fixes, the dates, and a polite request. Short and factual works best.

Should I mention that I am a legitimate business?

Briefly, if relevant, but it does not replace evidence. Operators care about what you fixed, not about how legitimate you feel.

Can I request removal for an IP I do not own?

Only the network owner or their authorised administrator should request removal. Operators may ask you to prove you control the address.
