Blacklist vs Blocklist vs Denylist: What Is the Difference?

Blacklist, blocklist, and denylist are often used for the same thing, but the terms have different histories. Learn what each means and which to use.

The same idea, different words

In email and network security, blacklist, blocklist, and denylist usually refer to the same thing: a list of addresses, domains, or senders that should be blocked or treated with suspicion. A whitelist, allowlist, or safelist is the opposite: a list of trusted senders that should always be accepted. For many years blacklist and whitelist were the standard terms, and they remain the most widely recognised, especially in the context of DNS blacklists, where the technical term is DNSBL. In recent years many organisations have moved to blocklist and allowlist, partly because the older terms are seen as carrying unhelpful associations, and partly because blocklist describes the function more plainly.

Why the terminology changed

The shift to blocklist and allowlist began in large technology companies and standards bodies that wanted language focused on what a list does rather than on colour metaphors. The Internet Engineering Task Force and several major vendors now prefer blocklist and allowlist in documentation. The change is a matter of style and inclusion rather than technology: a DNSBL works the same way whatever you call it. Some long-established projects, including Spamhaus, still use blacklist in their names and documentation, so the older term is unlikely to disappear soon. What matters in practice is that everyone in a conversation means the same thing, so define your terms when you write a policy or a report.

DNSBL, RBL, and URI blacklist

You will also meet a few related technical terms. DNSBL stands for DNS-based blacklist, the general name for lists queried through DNS. RBL stands for Real-time Blackhole List, an older name for the same concept that comes from the first such list, the MAPS RBL. URI blacklist, or URIBL, is a list of domains that appear in spam messages, rather than a list of sending IP addresses. A message can be clean on every IP list and still be filtered because a domain in its body is on a URI list. Knowing these terms helps you read checker results accurately, because a tool may report IP lists and domain lists separately.

How the terms are used in tools

Most blacklist checkers still use the word blacklist in their name and interface, because that is the term users search for. Our own site does the same. Inside the results, you may see blocklist used as a synonym, and some newer tools use denylist throughout. None of this changes the check itself: the tool queries DNS zones, reads the return codes, and reports which lists include the address. When you submit a delisting request, use the operator's own terminology from its website so your request is routed correctly. If a form asks for a blocklist removal, it means the same process as a blacklist removal.

Which term should you use?

Use blocklist and allowlist in new documentation, policies, and user-facing text, because that is the direction the industry is moving and it is the clearest wording. Keep blacklist in mind as a search term and a legacy name, since most existing lists, tools, and guides still use it. In a team, pick one pair of terms and use them consistently so nobody confuses a blocklist entry with a general spam score. The substance is what counts: a listing means an address has been flagged for abuse, whatever the label, and it needs investigation and a fix.

Frequently asked questions

Is a denylist the same as a blacklist?

Yes, in security contexts. Denylist is another newer synonym for a list of blocked addresses or domains.

What is the opposite of a blacklist?

A whitelist, also called an allowlist or safelist: a list of trusted senders that should always be accepted.

Does the term affect how a list works?

No. The technology is identical. The terms differ only in wording and in the conventions of the organisation using them.
