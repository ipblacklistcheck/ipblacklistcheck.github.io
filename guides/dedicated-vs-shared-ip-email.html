Dedicated vs Shared IP for Email: Which Should You Choose?

A dedicated IP gives you control; a shared IP gives you shared reputation. Compare costs, warm-up, risks, and which option fits your sending volume.

What a shared IP means

A shared IP is used by many senders at once, usually through a hosting provider or an email service that pools its customers. Your mail goes out from the same address as dozens or hundreds of other senders, and receivers judge the whole pool together. The advantage is cost and simplicity: the provider manages the IP, warms it up, and handles reputation for the group. The disadvantage is that you inherit the behaviour of strangers. One bad sender in the pool can damage delivery for everyone, and you have no control over who else uses the address.

What a dedicated IP means

A dedicated IP is used only by you. Your reputation is yours alone, for better or worse. If you send clean, wanted mail, your reputation improves steadily and you are never harmed by a neighbour. If you send spam or mail a bad list, the damage is also yours alone, and there is nobody else to dilute it. A dedicated IP costs more, usually as an add-on to a mail service, and it requires a careful warm-up period of several weeks during which you send small volumes to engaged recipients. It is not a shortcut to good delivery; it is a tool for senders who already have good practices.

Who should use a shared IP

Shared IPs suit small senders, new businesses, and anyone sending under roughly one hundred thousand messages a month. At that volume, a shared pool from a reputable provider usually has a better reputation than a new dedicated IP, because the pool has a long history and high volume. Shared IPs also suit senders who do not want to manage warm-up or monitor reputation closely. The key is to choose a reputable provider that monitors its pools, acts quickly on abuse, and separates transactional mail from marketing mail, because mixing the two in one pool creates unnecessary risk.

Who should use a dedicated IP

Dedicated IPs suit larger senders, typically above one hundred thousand messages a month, who can sustain enough volume to maintain the IP's reputation on their own. They also suit senders with strict deliverability needs, such as transactional mail where every message matters, and senders whose brand reputation is sensitive to sharing an address with unknown senders. If you choose a dedicated IP, commit to the warm-up schedule, keep your list clean, and monitor reputation weekly. A dedicated IP that is warmed up badly can perform worse than a good shared pool.

Making the decision

Compare your monthly volume, your budget, and how much email matters to your business. If you send little mail and want simplicity, a reputable shared pool is the sensible choice. If you send a lot, need control, and can follow a warm-up plan, a dedicated IP is worth the cost. Either way, the fundamentals do not change: authenticate your mail, mail only people who asked, keep your list clean, and monitor blacklists and complaint rates. The IP choice amplifies good or bad practices; it does not replace them.

If you are still unsure, start with a shared IP from a reputable provider and revisit the question when your volume grows. You can move to a dedicated IP later, once you have the sending history and the monitoring routine to support it. The wrong choice is rarely fatal, but the right choice, made for your actual volume rather than for an imagined future, saves money and protects delivery from the start.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a dedicated IP cost?

It varies by provider, often as a monthly add-on. Compare the cost against the value of reliable delivery for your business before deciding.

How long does warm-up take?

Typically four to eight weeks, starting with small volumes to engaged recipients and increasing gradually as metrics stay healthy.

Can I switch back to shared later?

Yes. Most providers let you change, but a switch means a new sending IP, so expect a short adjustment period while reputation builds.
