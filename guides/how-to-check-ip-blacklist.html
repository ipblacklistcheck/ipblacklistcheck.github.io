How to Check If Your IP Is Blacklisted (Step by Step)

Check your IP against public DNS blacklists in minutes. Follow this step-by-step method, learn what each result means, and know what to do next.

Start with your sending IP, not your website IP

Before you run any check, confirm which IP address actually sends your email. Many people check the IP of their website and get a clean result while their mail server uses a completely different address. Look in the headers of a message you sent, in your hosting or mail provider dashboard, or ask your provider which outbound IP your mail uses. If you send mail through a third-party service, that service's IP is the one that matters for delivery. Checking the wrong address wastes time and gives false reassurance. Write the correct IP down, and if you use more than one outbound server, check every one of them.

Run a check against several public lists

Enter the IP into a blacklist checker that queries multiple DNS blacklists, such as Spamhaus Zen, SpamCop, SORBS, and the Composite Blocking List. A good checker shows each list separately so you can see exactly where you are listed and where you are clean. Do not rely on a single list. One clean result means little if five other lists flag you, and one listing on a minor list may matter less than a listing on a major one. Our homepage checker uses DNS-over-HTTPS to query well-known lists directly from your browser, and it labels every result as indicative rather than definitive. A dedicated reputation service can check more lists, but a multi-list check is the right first step.

Read the result carefully

A result of listed means the list operator currently has your address in its database. Note the list name, because each operator has its own removal process. A result of not listed means the address was not found on that list at the time of the query, which is good news but not a guarantee for tomorrow. Lists change constantly, and a server can be added within hours of a spam outbreak. If the checker shows an error or timeout for one list, treat that list as unknown rather than clean, and retry it later. Keep a dated record of your results, including screenshots, so you can show a list operator or your hosting provider exactly what you saw and when.

Check the domain too

IP blacklists are only half the story. Domains can also be listed on URI blacklists when they appear in spam messages or host malicious content. If your mail is being rejected even though your IP is clean, check the domain in your links and your sending domain as well. A listed domain in a message body can cause the whole message to be filtered, even from a clean IP. Check the domains used in your email signatures, your website links, and any redirect or tracking domains you use. If a domain is listed, the fix is similar: remove the cause, clean any compromised pages, and request removal from the domain list operator.

What to do after the check

If everything is clean, set up regular monitoring so a future listing is caught early, ideally a weekly check and an alert if your mail volume or bounce rate changes suddenly. If you find a listing, do not panic and do not immediately request removal. First look at your mail logs for unusual volume, failed logins, or new scripts, and fix whatever you find. Then follow each operator's delisting process, one list at a time, and keep notes on every request you submit. Most removals take hours to a few days, and some lists delist automatically once the bad traffic stops. Recheck after removal and keep monitoring for at least a month.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I check my IP?

Weekly is a sensible minimum for anyone who sends business email. Check immediately if delivery drops, bounces rise, or a recipient reports your mail in spam.

Is a free blacklist checker accurate?

It is accurate for the lists it queries at that moment, but no free checker covers every list. Treat results as indicative and use a paid reputation service for a full audit.

Can I check someone else's IP?

Yes. Blacklist data is public, and checking any IP address you have a legitimate reason to investigate is normal practice for mail administrators.
