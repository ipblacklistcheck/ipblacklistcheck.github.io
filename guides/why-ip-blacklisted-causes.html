Why Your IP Got Blacklisted: The 8 Most Common Causes

The eight most common reasons IPs get blacklisted, from compromised mailboxes to shared hosting neighbours, and how to identify which one affected you.

Compromised mailboxes and weak passwords

The single most common cause of a listing is a mailbox that someone else has taken over. Attackers try common passwords and leaked credentials against mail servers around the clock, and once they get in they send thousands of spam messages before anyone notices. The owner often discovers the problem only when customers complain that mail is missing or when a blacklist check comes back positive. Change the password, enable two-factor authentication, and check for forwarding rules the attacker may have added.

Malware and hacked websites

A hacked website can send spam through its contact form, its mail function, or a hidden script the attacker uploaded. Content management systems with outdated plugins are a frequent target, and the spam may run for weeks before it is noticed because the site itself still looks normal to visitors. If your site was hacked, clean every infected file, update all software, change all passwords, and ask your host to review the server. A blacklist operator will want to see that the site is clean, not just that the spam has paused.

Open relays and misconfigured servers

A mail server that relays mail for anyone, known as an open relay, is quickly found by spammers and abused heavily. Modern mail software ships with relaying disabled by default, but a bad configuration change can open it up. Similarly, a server without reverse DNS, or with a generic hostname that looks like a home connection, is treated with suspicion. Check that your server only relays for authenticated users and your own domains, that it has a proper PTR record, and that it identifies itself with a real hostname. These basic settings are checked by many spam filters as well as by blacklist operators.

Bulk mail sent badly

Sending a newsletter or marketing campaign from a cold IP, to a purchased list, with no authentication and no unsubscribe link, is one of the fastest ways to get listed. Recipients mark the mail as spam, providers see complaint spikes, and list operators see the pattern. Legitimate bulk mail needs a warmed-up IP, a permission-based list, SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, a working unsubscribe, and a sending pace that matches your normal volume. If you buy email lists, expect complaints and listings, because those addresses never agreed to hear from you. Build your list properly instead.

Shared hosting neighbours

On shared hosting, dozens or hundreds of customers share one outbound IP address. If one of them sends spam, the whole IP can be listed and every other customer suffers, even though they did nothing wrong. This is frustrating because you cannot fix another customer's behaviour directly. Your options are to ask the host to move the offender or to move you to a cleaner IP, or to move your mail to a dedicated IP or a reputable mail service. When choosing a host, ask how they handle abuse and whether mail uses a separate, monitored IP pool.

No authentication: missing SPF, DKIM, and DMARC

Mail without SPF, DKIM, and DMARC is easy to forge, and filters treat unauthenticated mail with suspicion. If someone forges your domain to send spam, recipients may report your domain, and list operators may flag the IP that the forged mail appeared to come from. Publishing correct authentication records does not guarantee delivery, but it gives receivers a reliable way to tell your real mail from forgeries, and it is now expected by major providers. Check your records with a DNS lookup tool and fix any errors, including the common mistake of having more than one SPF record.

Sudden volume spikes and new IPs

A server that normally sends fifty messages a day and suddenly sends fifty thousand looks like a spam outbreak, even if the mail is legitimate. The same applies to a brand-new IP with no sending history. Reputation is built gradually, so warm up new addresses slowly, spread large campaigns over several days, and keep your daily volume reasonably steady.

User complaints and spam traps

When many recipients mark your mail as spam, providers notice and may report the sending IP to list operators. Spam traps, old addresses that were never real subscribers but were harvested from the web, are another trigger. Hitting a trap suggests your list was bought or scraped rather than built with permission. Keep your list clean and honour unsubscribes immediately.

Frequently asked questions

Can I be listed even if I never sent spam?

Yes. A previous tenant of your IP, a neighbour on shared hosting, or someone forging your domain can all cause a listing without any spam from you.

Which cause should I check first?

Check your mail logs and sent-mail folders for unexpected volume. That usually points to a compromised account or script within minutes.

Will fixing the cause remove the listing automatically?

Some lists delist automatically once bad traffic stops, but most require a request. Fix first, then request removal from each operator.
